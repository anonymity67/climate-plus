# Pilot, sustainability and venture development

## Initial deployment unit

Start with one professional operator or NGO managing a small cluster of community water points. The product's job is to help staff inspect evidence, plan feasible responses, coordinate work and communicate confirmed service. Households and schools are beneficiaries; an institution is the payment candidate. No partner or customer is confirmed.

Existing systems such as SafePani and mWater are potential integration contexts, not evidence of partnerships. Climate++ should complement authorized asset and observation records rather than demand that an operator rebuild its entire information system.

## Six-to-eight-week pilot preparation

| Stage | Work | Completion evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery | Five operator interviews, three institutional-buyer interviews and five resident/collection-volunteer conversations | Dated notes, actual workflow, input burden, decision authority and alternative tools |
| Site selection | Agree one service boundary, responsible counterpart and intended uses | Written permission, operator contact, data responsibilities and safety process |
| Baseline | Observe scheduling, stock, interruptions, communications and sampling practice | Measured records, missing-data register, calibration and source definitions |
| Shadow operation | Generate recommendations without directing real service | Two weeks of reconciled predictions and operator feasibility judgments |
| Review | Compare with simple practice under equal information/resources | Error, workload, false alerts, infeasible recommendations and who would gain/lose |
| Supervised trial | Only after operator and domain review, use approved recommendations | Measured outcomes, deviations, complaints and independently checked execution |

This is a staged plan, not a claim that a six-week small pilot can establish health effects or national demand.

## Required responsibilities

The operating organization owns source clearance, public service commitments and field execution. A qualified water-domain reviewer approves objectives, sampling and interpretation. A laboratory or competent testing service owns analytical methods and QA. A field lead owns sample identity, calibration and custody. The software team owns data handling, reproducibility, defects and versioned changes. A community counterpart reviews accessibility and allocation priorities.

## Field observation checklist

Record station ID, WGS84 coordinates and accuracy, water-body/reach, sample date/time/timezone, depth, weather, recent rainfall, tide/flow where relevant, sampler, instrument identifier and calibration, parameter, unit and chemical form, method, detection/quantification limit, qualifiers, replicate type and original result. Keep missing and censored values explicit.

For laboratory samples add bottle/preservative requirements from the chosen method, sample IDs, collection and receipt times, custody transfers, transport conditions, blank/duplicate records, laboratory report, method-specific holding-time compliance and reviewer sign-off. Do not use an invented universal holding time.

For source investigation obtain permission; identify the actual drainage path; collect comparable upstream, outfall and downstream evidence; measure flow; assess mixing and travel time; inspect relevant operating/treatment records; repeat under representative conditions. Escalate suspected unlawful discharge through the responsible authority rather than publishing an unsupported allegation.

## Pilot metrics and decision rules

| Question | Metric | Comparison and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Is the water ledger reliable? | Absolute stock error, missing observations, reconciliation discrepancies | Compare with independent measured stock; define an acceptable tolerance with the operator |
| Are plans executable? | Feasible tasks, confirmed deliveries, missed commitments, staff minutes | Compare to existing scheduling and simple threshold rules |
| Does service improve? | Essential-demand shortfall, outage duration, failed trips, collection time and household cost | Measure each; do not convert simulations into benefits |
| Is allocation acceptable? | Coverage by catchment, accessibility and priority objections | Review the worst-served group and who loses under each policy |
| Does investigation improve? | Useful samples per budget, time to verified pathway, false leads | Compare with highest-concentration-first or current practice |
| Is mitigation effective? | Matched upstream/downstream concentration and load before/after | Account for flow, seasonality and other simultaneous changes |

Before the trial, predeclare how missing data, school closures, delivery failures and unexpected events are handled. Keep development and evaluation periods separate. Stop acting on a model when it is outside its domain, inputs are stale or field results fail the agreed tolerances.

## Environmental implications

Better service continuity must not be purchased through unexamined environmental damage. Track water withdrawal, pumping energy, treatment consumables, laboratory waste, sludge and RO reject/brine where relevant. Count pollutant load as well as concentration so dilution is not mistaken for pollution removal. Visible-trash collection does not establish removal of dissolved contaminants or pathogens. Aeration alone does not resolve toxic or fecal contamination.

The current prototype reports no avoided emissions. A future emissions calculation must state activity data, geographic electricity/fuel factors, boundaries, source dates and uncertainty. Check rebound effects if improved access increases extraction.

## Economics and scale

The accompanying workbook is an editable planning sketch, not market evidence. It separates a USD 1,000 pilot-preparation envelope from illustrative institutional software economics. It excludes infrastructure, routine water delivery, major repairs and founder compensation; those exclusions must not disappear from a funding discussion.

Test institutional onboarding/support fees and cluster subscriptions only after observing measurable operator value and willingness to pay. Keep resident notices free. Where institutions cannot fund the service, evaluate supported procurement with explicit ongoing subsidy and verification costs. Do not base the model on speculative tokens or automatic payments from unverified reports.

Scale in gates: one repeatable cluster, multiple district operators, an institutionally supported national programme, then partner-led international pilots. Each stage requires onboarding cost, support capacity, data agreements, local-language usability, domain calibration and sustainable payment. Geography alone is not the scaling strategy.

## Founder and portfolio evidence

Retain the source history, data cards, research notebooks/scripts, failed hypotheses, test outputs, user-study notes and decisions changed by evidence. A strong portfolio case study explains why supplied pollution labels were not used, why RONI did not earn a superiority claim, how numerical parity was checked, and where an operator can override priorities. Attribute all data and methods and disclose AI assistance.
